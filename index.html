How to read the chart

Competency shortage risk is an estimate, not a measured shortage: how likely a trust is to run short of staff able to cover a competency, given its patients and staff mix.

One row = one trust. The three panels describe the same trust in three ways. Sort the rows with the buttons, and hover a row for its raw numbers.

(a) Role-only, the grey bar. How many patients are waiting per theatre staff member, compared with the average across the 88 trusts. The thin vertical line is that average. A bar that ends past the line means more waiting patients per staff member than average. This is the headcount-only view, so it says nothing about competencies.

(b) and (c), the coloured squares. Each row has five squares, one per competency group, in the same order: invasive monitoring, difficult airway, regional/positioning, children, obstetric/gynae. Hover a column heading for its full name. The colour is the trust's rank among the 88 trusts for that competency group. Pale means lower competency shortage risk, dark red means higher. It is a rank, not an amount, so a dark square means "among the trusts at higher shortage risk for this competency", not "short by a set number of people".

What to look for. If the three panels agreed, the dark squares would line up with the long grey bars. They don't. Take King's College Hospital: it has the longest grey bar, Hypothesis 1 shows it at high shortage risk on invasive monitoring and children but not on regional/positioning, and Hypothesis 2 shows dark red across all five. Alder Hey Children's has a short grey bar but is at the very top for children's competencies under Hypothesis 1. Headcount alone would miss that.

Mind the limits. Squares in the same column are comparable between trusts, but a colour in (b) and a colour in (c) answer different questions, because they rest on different assumptions. In (c) the five squares usually look alike because the made-up weights only separate staff by grade.

The two hypotheses in plain words

Open data tells us how many staff of each type a hospital has, and how many patients are waiting for each kind of operation. It does not tell us which specific competencies each person has. Your competency ontology would. Until then we fill that gap in two opposite ways and see what each shows.

Hypothesis 1: the patients differ, the staff are the same

Imagine every trust has an identical team. The only difference is what its patients need. A children's hospital has lots of children waiting, so it needs more children's competencies. A hospital with many hip and knee operations needs more nerve-block and patient-positioning competencies.

How it is worked out: each specialty's waiting list is turned into demand for five competency groups, using my guessed links. A trust's score is its share of demand for a competency compared with the national share.

What it tells us: where local demand points to a particular competency. It gives sensible answers: the children's hospitals come top for children's competencies.

Hypothesis 2: the staff differ, the patients are the same

Imagine every trust has an identical set of patients. The only difference is who works there. A team with many senior, specialist staff can cover harder competencies. A team of mostly junior staff can cover fewer.

How it is worked out: each grade of staff is given a guessed level of coverage for each competency group. A trust's score is how thinly its staff cover a competency compared with the national average.

What it tells us: only how senior the team is. We have no data on who holds which competency, so all five competency groups move together.

Why two? They are opposite extremes, and the real world is a mix. If both give a different picture from the plain headcount view, and from each other, headcount alone is hiding something.

Sources and limits

Where the numbers come from.
Staff: NHS England Workforce Statistics, August 2026. These are built from the Electronic Staff Record (ESR), the NHS payroll and HR system. I counted full-time-equivalent (FTE) staff in two groups only: ODPs by job role and grade (general, specialist, advanced, manager), and registered nurses whose area of work is recorded as Anaesthetics, by pay band. Nurses working in theatres but recorded under another area (for example scrub or recovery nurses) and all other nurses are not counted. ODP totals reconcile to the published England figures.
Demand: NHS Referral to Treatment (RTT) waiting-list data, August 2026. I used patients already waiting for an operation or procedure (a decision to admit has been made), by specialty, as a stand-in for theatre work coming. Obstetrics is not in this data, so obstetric/gynae demand is only a gynaecology proxy. Emergency and remote work is not captured. Only trusts with at least 1,500 such patients and 40 theatre staff FTE are shown.
(a) Role-only: waiting patients divided by theatre staff FTE, relative to the average across the 88 trusts (1.0). It sees headcount and nothing about competencies.
(b) Hypothesis 1: assumes staff competencies are spread evenly, and asks which competencies each trust's own waiting list calls for. (c) Hypothesis 2: assumes demand is spread evenly, and asks which competencies each trust's staff mix (seniority and grade) can cover.
Assumptions made by me, not validated by clinicians: the link from each specialty to five competency groups (invasive monitoring, difficult airway, regional/positioning, children, obstetric/gynae), and how much experience each grade is assumed to bring to each competency group. Hypothesis 2 mostly reflects seniority only, because its weights differ only by grade, so its five columns move together. Colours show each trust's rank (0 to 100) among the 88 trusts within a competency group, not an absolute level. One month of data. Illustrative, not for operational use.
